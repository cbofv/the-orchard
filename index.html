How to revise

The Guide

Revise less, remember more. Pick a tab, or start at the beginning.

Most revision time goes on things that feel productive but don’t stick. Everything here comes back to three rules.

01Recall itPull it out of your head. Don’t just read it again.
02Space itCome back after a gap, just as you’re starting to forget.
03Apply itAnswer real exam questions and mark them like an examiner.

How to revise any topic

  1. Check you understand it. If it doesn’t make sense yet, fix that first with your class notes, a revision guide or a short video. Memorising something you don’t understand rarely sticks.
  2. Blurt. Close everything and write down all you know about the topic in 5–10 minutes.
  3. Check and fill gaps. Compare with your notes. Add what you missed in a different colour. The gaps are what you revise.
  4. Turn gaps into flashcards. One question per card, short answers. Ask “why?” on a few of them.
  5. Do exam questions. Find past-paper questions on that topic and answer them without notes.
  6. Mark strictly. Use the mark scheme, and the examiner’s report if there is one. Note the exact words that earn marks.
  7. Rate it. Green, amber or red, then log it in the Study log.
  8. Schedule the comeback. Review it again after about 1, 3, 7 and 21 days. Red topics come back sooner.

Skip these

They feel like work, but research rates them among the least effective uses of time.

RereadingFeels familiar, so you think you know it. Recognising is not the same as recalling.
HighlightingFine for spotting key points the first time, useless as revision on its own.
Copying notesRewriting neatly takes hours and barely tests memory. Write from memory instead.
Pretty notesTime spent on colours and layout is time not spent recalling.
CrammingWorks for tomorrow, fades within days. GCSEs need it to last until summer.
All-nightersSleep is when memories get stored. Losing it undoes the revision you just did.

A day that works

10mFlashcards due today, across any subjects.
25mOne focused block: blurt a topic or do past-paper questions. Phone in another room.
5mBreak. Move, drink water, no scrolling.
25mSecond block on a different subject or topic.
5mMark and log. Rate topics and note what comes back tomorrow.

About an hour on a school night. Add blocks at weekends and in holidays. Doing a little every day beats long weekend sessions.

Each week

  1. Plan which topics to cover, starting with red ones and anything due for a spaced review.
  2. Mix subjects across the week rather than doing one subject a day.
  3. Do at least one timed past paper or long section, closer to exams one per subject each week.
  4. Look back at the Study log and Grades: where are the gaps to target?

Your brain stores what you learn while you rest. Look after it.

Before and on exam day

  1. Last week: past papers under timed conditions, then flashcards for anything you dropped marks on. No brand-new topics the night before.
  2. Night before: a light review of key flashcards, pack your bag, then a normal bedtime.
  3. Morning: breakfast, water, and a quick look at a few formulas or quotes, not a full topic.
  4. In the exam: read the question twice, underline the command word, check the marks and give that many points. Plan longer answers for a minute first.
  5. Stuck? Move on and come back. Write something for every question; partial marks count.
  6. Leave time to check units, working and that you answered what was asked.

Getting the most from a past paper

  1. Real conditions. Timed, closed book, no phone.
  2. Mark it properly. Use the official mark scheme, and be strict.
  3. Find the grade. Look up the grade boundaries for that exam series.
  4. Fix the gaps. Rate the topics you lost marks on red, and redo those questions a week later.
  5. Read the examiner report. It shows the common mistakes on each question.
  6. Space them out. One paper per subject every week or two works better than cramming several at once.
